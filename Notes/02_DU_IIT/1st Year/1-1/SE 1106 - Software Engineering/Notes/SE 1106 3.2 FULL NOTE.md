# 3.2 People — Full Note

---

## 3.2.1 The Players

Five constituencies populate the software process:

| # | Player | Role |
|---|--------|------|
| 1 | **Senior Managers** | Define business issues that significantly influence the project |
| 2 | **Project (Technical) Managers** | Plan, motivate, organize, and control the practitioners |
| 3 | **Practitioners** | Deliver the technical skills needed to engineer the product |
| 4 | **Customers** | Specify requirements; other stakeholders have peripheral interest in the outcome |
| 5 | **End-Users** | Interact with the software once released for production use |

- The team must be organized to **maximize each person's skills and abilities**
- This is the job of the **team leader**

---

## 3.2.2 Team Leaders

### MOI Model of Leadership (by Jerry Weinberg)

| Trait | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **M — Motivation** | Ability to encourage (push or pull) technical people to produce to their best ability |
| **O — Organization** | Ability to mold existing processes (or invent new ones) to translate concept into final product |
| **I — Ideas / Innovation** | Ability to encourage creativity even within established bounds |

- Weinberg suggests leaders should apply a **problem-solving management style**
  - Concentrate on understanding the problem
  - Manage the flow of ideas
  - Communicate that **quality counts and will not be compromised**

### Four Key Traits of Effective Project Managers (by Edgemon)

| Trait | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Problem Solving** | Diagnose technical & organizational issues; structure solutions; apply lessons from past projects; remain flexible |
| **Managerial Identity** | Take charge; have confidence to assume control; allow good people to follow their instincts |
| **Achievement** | Reward initiative and accomplishment; demonstrate that controlled risk-taking will not be punished |
| **Influence & Team Building** | "Read" people — understand verbal and nonverbal signals; remain under control in high-stress situations |

## 3.2.3 The Software Team

### Three Ways to Apply Human Resources (n people, k years)

| Option | Structure | Coordination |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Option 1** | n individuals → m tasks; little combined work | By software manager (who may have 6+ other projects) |
| **Option 2** | n individuals → m tasks (m < n); informal "teams" with ad hoc leader | Among teams by software manager |
| **Option 3** | n individuals → t formal teams; each team has defined structure | By both the team and the project manager |

- **Option 3 (formal team organization) is the most productive**

---

### Mantei's Three Generic Team Organizations

| Structure | Leader | Decision Making | Communication |
|-----------|--------|----------------|---------------|
| **DD — Democratic Decentralized** | No permanent leader; rotating task coordinators | Group consensus | Horizontal |
| **CD — Controlled Decentralized** | Defined leader + secondary leaders for subtasks | Group activity for problem solving; leader partitions implementation | Horizontal among subgroups + Vertical along hierarchy |
| **CC — Controlled Centralized** | Single team leader manages problem solving & coordination | By the team leader | Vertical (leader ↔ members) |

### Seven Project Factors for Team Structure (by Mantei)

1. Difficulty of the problem
2. Size of the program (LOC or function points)
3. Team lifetime (how long the team stays together)
4. Degree of modularity of the problem
5. Required quality and reliability
6. Rigidity of the delivery date
7. Degree of sociability (communication) required

### Which Team Structure Fits Which Situation?

| Factor | Best Structure | Reasoning |
|--------|---------------|-----------|
| **Simple problems** | CC or CD | Centralized structure completes tasks faster |
| **Difficult problems** | DD | Decentralized teams generate more & better solutions |
| **Very large projects** | CC or CD | Performance is inversely proportional to communication volume; subgrouping needed |
| **Long team lifetime** | DD | Results in high morale and job satisfaction |
| **Low modularity** | DD | Higher communication volume is needed |
| **High modularity** | CC or CD | People can work independently |
| **Fewer defects** | CC or CD | Produce fewer defects (also depends on QA activities) |
| **High sociability required** | DD | Decentralized teams are best for high sociability |
| **Speed (shortest time)** | CC or CD | Decentralized teams generally take more time |

### Constantine's Four Organizational Paradigms

| Paradigm | Description | Best For | Weakness |
|----------|-------------|----------|----------|
| **Closed** | Traditional hierarchy of authority (similar to CC) | Software similar to past efforts | Less likely to be innovative |
| **Random** | Loosely structured; depends on individual initiative | Innovation or technological breakthrough | Struggles with "orderly performance" |
| **Open** | Collaborative; heavy communication; consensus-based decisions; mix of closed control + random innovation | Complex problems | May not perform as efficiently |
| **Synchronous** | Natural compartmentalization; members work on pieces with little active communication | Problems that can be naturally divided | Limited communication |

### Chief Programmer Team (Historical — earliest CC/CD structure)

- Proposed by **Harlan Mills**, described by **Baker**
- Nucleus:
  - **Senior Engineer (Chief Programmer)** — plans, coordinates, reviews all technical activities
  - **Technical Staff** (2–5 people) — conduct analysis and development
  - **Backup Engineer** — supports and can replace the chief programmer with minimal loss of continuity
- May also include:
  - Specialists (e.g., telecom expert, DB designer)
  - Support staff (e.g., tech writers, clerical)
  - **Software Librarian**

### Software Librarian Functions

- Maintains and controls all elements of the **software configuration** (documentation, source listings, data, storage media)
- Collects and formats **software productivity data**
- Catalogs and indexes **reusable software components**
- Assists teams in **research, evaluation, and document preparation**
- Acts as **controller, coordinator, and evaluator** of the software configuration

---

### Jelled Teams (by DeMarco & Lister — *Peopleware*)

- **Jelled team** = a group so strongly knit that **the whole is greater than the sum of the parts**
- Once a team jells → probability of success goes **way up**; the team becomes a "juggernaut for success"
- Members are **significantly more productive and more motivated**
- They share:
  - Common goal
  - Common culture
  - "Sense of eliteness"

### Requirements for High-Performance Teams

- Team members must have **trust** in one another
- Distribution of **skills** must be appropriate to the problem
- **Mavericks** may have to be excluded if team cohesiveness is to be maintained

### Jackman's Five Team Toxicity Factors & Antitoxins

| # | Toxin | Antitoxin |
|---|-------|-----------|
| 1 | **Frenzied work atmosphere** — members waste energy and lose focus | Ensure team has all needed info; don't modify major goals unless necessary; deliver bad news early |
| 2 | **High frustration** — caused by personal, business, or tech factors | Give the team as much decision-making responsibility as possible |
| 3 | **Fragmented / poorly coordinated procedures** — poorly defined or improperly chosen process model | (1) Ensure software characteristics match the rigor of the process; (2) Allow the team to choose its own process |
| 4 | **Unclear roles** — lack of accountability → finger-pointing | Clearly refine roles & responsibilities before the project begins; team sets its own accountability mechanisms (e.g., formal technical reviews) |
| 5 | **Continuous exposure to failure** — loss of confidence, lower morale | Establish team-based feedback & problem solving; failure by one member = failure by the team → team-oriented corrective action |

## 3.2.4 Coordination and Communication Issues

### Why Software Projects Get Into Trouble

- **Scale** — large efforts → complexity, confusion, difficulty coordinating
- **Uncertainty** — continuing stream of changes
- **Interoperability** — new software must communicate with existing software and conform to predefined constraints

### Kraul & Streeter's Project Coordination Techniques

| Technique | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Formal, impersonal** | SE documents & deliverables, source code, technical memos, milestones, schedules, project control tools, change requests, error tracking reports, repository data |
| **Formal, interpersonal** | QA activities — status review meetings, design & code inspections |
| **Informal, interpersonal** | Group meetings for info dissemination & problem solving; co-location of requirements and development staff |
| **Electronic communication** | Email, electronic bulletin boards, video-based conferencing |
| **Interpersonal networking** | Informal discussions with team members and those outside the project |

### Key Findings (from study of 65 projects)

- **Interpersonal networking** was rated the technique with the **highest coordination and communication value**
- Early QA mechanisms (**requirements & design reviews**) were perceived to have **more value** than later evaluations (**code inspections**)
